# Indian Personal Finance Knowledge Base

Curated reference corpus for the advisor's retrieval step. Each `##` section is one
chunk. Content covers durable rules and conventions, deliberately excluding live
rates -- those are fetched at runtime by the research crew. Verify statutory limits
against the primary source before acting, since Finance Acts change annually.

## Order of financial priorities
Money should be deployed in this sequence, because a later step fails if an earlier one is skipped. First, cover essential living expenses. Second, clear high-interest debt such as credit card revolving balances and personal loans, where the interest rate typically exceeds any realistic investment return. Third, build an emergency fund. Fourth, buy term life insurance and health insurance. Fifth, invest for long-term goals. Sixth, spend on lifestyle. Starting an equity SIP before having an emergency fund usually leads to redeeming that SIP at a loss during the first crisis.

## Emergency fund sizing
An emergency fund should hold six months of essential expenses including EMIs, as a default. Three months can suffice for a dual-income household with very stable government or large-corporate jobs. Twelve months is more appropriate for a single earner, a freelancer, a business owner, or someone in a volatile sector. The fund must be liquid and capital-stable: a sweep-in fixed deposit, a liquid mutual fund, or a plain savings account. It must not sit in equity, because emergencies and market crashes correlate. Its purpose is availability, not return, so a low interest rate is acceptable.

## Term life insurance
Life cover is only needed by people whose income others depend on. The standard guidance is a sum assured of ten to fifteen times annual income, adjusted upward for outstanding home loans and young children, and downward for existing assets. Buy pure term insurance. Avoid endowment plans, money-back policies and ULIPs sold as insurance-cum-investment: they typically deliver four to six percent returns while bundling inadequate cover, and mixing the two objectives makes both worse. Policy term should extend to the expected retirement age. Disclose all medical history, because non-disclosure is the leading cause of claim rejection.

## Health insurance
Hold personal health insurance independent of employer group cover, because employer cover disappears with the job and often exactly when it is most needed. A floater of at least ten lakh rupees is a reasonable base in metro cities, extended cheaply with a super top-up policy that kicks in above a deductible. Note the waiting periods: pre-existing conditions are usually covered only after two to four years, so buying early matters. A single hospitalisation can consume years of accumulated SIP contributions, which is why protection ranks above investing in the priority order.

## Asset allocation and the age glidepath
A common starting heuristic sets the equity share of a portfolio at one hundred minus the investor's age, with the remainder in debt and a small gold allocation. This is a starting point, not a rule. It should be adjusted for risk tolerance, income stability and, most importantly, goal horizon. Money needed within three years does not belong in equity at all, regardless of age. Money that will not be touched for fifteen years or more can carry a high equity share. Rebalance roughly annually back to target weights: this mechanically sells what has run up and buys what has lagged.

## Equity investing vehicles
Broad-market index funds tracking the Nifty 50 or a total-market index are the default core holding, because their expense ratios are typically around 0.1 to 0.2 percent versus 1 to 2 percent for active funds, and that gap compounds. Flexi-cap funds allow a manager discretion across market capitalisations. Mid-cap and small-cap funds are more volatile and can fall fifty to sixty percent in a drawdown; higher risk raises the dispersion of outcomes but does not guarantee a higher return, so they suit only long horizons and strong stomachs. Always buy direct plans rather than regular plans: direct plans exclude distributor commission and save roughly 0.5 to 1 percent per year.

## Systematic investment plans
A SIP invests a fixed amount at a fixed interval, which averages the purchase price across market levels and removes the need to time entries. For a lump sum, historical evidence generally favours investing immediately over staggering, since markets rise more often than they fall, but staggering over three to six months is a reasonable behavioural compromise for a nervous investor. The strongest predictor of SIP success is not fund selection but not stopping during a downturn, which is precisely when units are cheapest.

## Public Provident Fund
The PPF accepts between five hundred and one lakh fifty thousand rupees per financial year. Its interest rate is reset quarterly by the Ministry of Finance. It carries a sovereign guarantee and enjoys exempt-exempt-exempt tax treatment: contributions, interest and maturity proceeds are all tax-free. The lock-in is fifteen years, extendable in five-year blocks, with limited partial withdrawal permitted from the seventh year. The lock-in is the real cost, so PPF suits the portion of a debt allocation that genuinely will not be needed for a long time.

## Employees' Provident Fund
An employee contributes twelve percent of basic salary to the EPF and the employer matches it, with part of the employer share going to the pension scheme. The interest rate is declared annually by the EPFO. Because EPF is deducted before salary is credited, it is already part of a salaried person's debt allocation and should be counted when assessing the overall equity-to-debt mix rather than treated as invisible. Interest earned on employee contributions above two lakh fifty thousand rupees per year is taxable. The Voluntary Provident Fund allows contributing more at the same rate.

## National Pension System
The NPS is a low-cost retirement vehicle with a choice of equity, corporate bond, government bond and alternative funds, subject to an equity cap. Its drawback is illiquidity until age sixty, and that a portion of the corpus must be annuitised at exit. On tax: the additional fifty thousand rupee deduction under section 80CCD(1B) is available only under the old tax regime. However, the employer's NPS contribution deduction under section 80CCD(2) remains available under the new regime, which makes employer-routed NPS one of the few tax levers left for new-regime taxpayers.

## New versus old tax regime
The new regime is the default. It offers wider slabs and lower rates but removes most deductions and exemptions, including section 80C, section 80D, and house rent allowance. The standard deduction for salaried taxpayers remains available under the new regime, as does the section 80CCD(2) deduction for employer NPS contributions. The old regime retains deductions and suits taxpayers with large genuine claims, typically substantial HRA plus a home loan plus a full 80C. The correct approach is to compute liability under both regimes with actual figures rather than follow a general rule. Critically, tax saving should never drive an otherwise bad investment: a poor product with a deduction usually loses to a good product without one.

## Section 80C and tax-saving instruments
Section 80C allows a deduction of up to one lakh fifty thousand rupees per year, but only under the old regime. Eligible items include EPF and PPF contributions, ELSS equity funds, life insurance premiums, principal repayment on a home loan, Sukanya Samriddhi accounts, and five-year tax-saving fixed deposits. Among these, ELSS has the shortest lock-in at three years and is equity-oriented. For a taxpayer on the new regime, section 80C is irrelevant, and ELSS loses its main advantage over an ordinary index fund.

## Capital gains taxation
Under the rules introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024, listed equity and equity mutual funds held longer than twelve months attract long-term capital gains tax at twelve and a half percent, with an annual exemption of one lakh twenty-five thousand rupees of gains; held twelve months or less, short-term gains are taxed at twenty percent. Debt mutual funds purchased on or after the first of April 2023 are treated as specified mutual funds: their gains are taxed at the investor's slab rate with no indexation benefit and no long-term concession. This tax asymmetry is a reason to favour EPF and PPF over debt funds for genuinely long-term debt allocation, since both are tax-free.

## Gold in a portfolio
Gold acts as a hedge and a currency-debasement diversifier rather than a growth engine, and a five to ten percent allocation is the usual guidance. Sovereign Gold Bonds, historically the best vehicle because they paid two and a half percent interest on top of the gold price, are no longer being issued, so gold exchange-traded funds and gold index funds are the practical route. They avoid the making charges, storage risk and purity uncertainty of physical jewellery, which should never be counted as an investment. Under the post-2024 rules, long-term gains on gold funds are taxed at twelve and a half percent after a twelve-month holding period.

## Debt repayment versus investing
Compare the loan's interest rate to the realistic after-tax return on the alternative investment. Credit card debt at thirty to forty percent annualised and personal loans in the mid-teens should always be repaid before investing, since no portfolio reliably beats that. Home loans in the eight to nine percent range are a genuine judgement call against an expected equity return of ten to twelve percent, and the answer depends on risk tolerance more than arithmetic. Total EMIs above forty percent of take-home pay indicate overleverage and argue for prepayment over new investment. Prepaying early in a loan's tenure saves far more interest than prepaying later, because early instalments are mostly interest.

## Inflation and real returns
A nominal return is meaningless without subtracting inflation. At six percent inflation, money loses roughly half its purchasing power in twelve years, so a fixed deposit yielding six and a half percent pre-tax is close to zero real return after tax for a higher-rate taxpayer. This is the core argument for holding equity over long horizons despite its volatility. The rule of seventy-two gives a quick estimate: dividing seventy-two by the annual return rate approximates the years needed to double an amount. Retirement planning in particular must be done in inflation-adjusted terms, because a target set in today's rupees will be badly wrong in thirty years.

## Common and expensive mistakes
Buying insurance as an investment through ULIPs or endowment plans. Choosing regular plans over direct plans and paying commission indefinitely. Chasing last year's top-performing fund, which reliably underperforms after mean reversion. Holding fifteen overlapping mutual funds and calling it diversification when three or four cover the whole market. Stopping SIPs during a crash. Trading futures and options, where the large majority of retail participants lose money. Keeping large idle balances in a savings account earning below inflation. Treating a self-occupied house as an investment when it produces no income and cannot be partially sold. Ignoring the expense ratio because the percentage looks small next to a compounding horizon of decades.
